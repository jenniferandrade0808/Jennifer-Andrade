# Economic Research Specification: Processing Labor Scarcity and Food Resilience on Kauai

## 1. Research Question and Hypotheses

**Research Question:** Why does Kauai depend on imported beef during normal market conditions, a structural vulnerability that maritime supply disruptions subsequently expose, despite maintaining extensive pasture acreage and a standing inventory of nearly 14,000 cattle?

**Primary Hypothesis (Sequential Constraints: Labor Binds First, Herd Binds Second):** Converting island cattle into retail-inspected beef is constrained by two sequential ceilings:

- **Constraint 1 (Binding Labor Ceiling):** Trained slaughterhouse and fabrication labor in USDA-inspected facilities is the primary binding constraint capping immediate throughput. High reservation wages driven by local housing costs, combined with wage competition from the resort and hospitality sector, create a relatively inelastic local labor supply for meat processing.
- **Constraint 2 (Biological Herd Ceiling):** Even if labor constraints were eliminated, the sustainable biological offtake of the island herd represents a secondary ceiling that satisfies only a fraction of aggregate island beef demand.

**Rival Hypothesis (The Calf-Export Supply Model):** The shortfall in local beef processing is driven by animal supply leakage rather than a processing labor bottleneck: ranchers rationally export weaned calves to mainland feedlots because higher net returns on the mainland starve local plants of harvestable livestock.

## 2. Falsification Conditions

The primary labor-scarcity hypothesis will be falsified if:

- **Condition 1 (Plant Idle Capacity and Livestock Shortage):** Empirical evidence or operational scheduling demonstrates that local USDA-inspected slaughter facilities maintain persistent idle labor capacity and open slaughter slots without booking backlogs, indicating that operational volume is constrained by a lack of cattle deliveries rather than cutting labor hands.
- **Condition 2 (Downstream Distribution Failure):** Post-disaster conversion breakdowns stem primarily from downstream transportation and logistics failures (road washouts, delivery vehicle fuel shortages, or downed electrical grid power disabling cold storage) rather than an operational ceiling on processing labor headcount.

**Baseline Food Import Benchmark:** Source: PingSun Leung and Matthew Loke (2008), University of Hawaii CTAHR, Economic Issues EI-16, establishing the statewide 85 to 90 percent food import baseline. Scoped explicitly as a statewide benchmark.

**Labor Market and Wage Differential Metrics:** Source: Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics (OEWS) for the Hawaii / Kauai nonmetropolitan area (comprising Hawaii and Kauai counties). Wage Data: Uses published May 2025 median hourly wages for Butchers and Meat Cutters (SOC 51-3021, $24.39) and Slaughterers and Meat Packers (SOC 51-3023, $18.90), contrasted against nonmetropolitan hospitality benchmarks (Food Preparation Workers at $20.65, Restaurant Cooks at $22.63, Housekeeping Cleaners at $24.34, Hotel Desk Clerks at $26.38, and Waiters/Waitresses at $30.62). Cost of Living Baseline: MIT Living Wage Calculator and DBEDT Kauai County Housing Metrics, establishing the local hourly reservation wage baseline required to meet basic living costs.

**Population and Beef Demand Base:** Source: DBEDT resident population (approx. 73,000) and daily visitor census (approx. 27,000), totaling 100,000 baseline consumers. Baseline Beef Consumption Rate: USDA Economic Research Service (ERS) per-capita beef consumption benchmark of 59 pounds retail weight per year, or roughly 1.13 pounds per week. Modeled Normal Island Beef Demand: 100,000 people times 1.13 pounds per week = approximately 113,000 pounds of retail beef per week.

**Carcass Yield Benchmark:** Assumes a standard grass-fed live harvest weight of 1,000 pounds, a 56 percent dressing percentage (560 pounds hanging weight), and a conservative 62.5 percent boneless retail cut yield (Penn State Extension / USDA ERS carcass benchmarks), yielding 350 pounds of retail-trimmed beef per head.

## 5. Course Frameworks Applied

**Microeconomics (Labor Market Clearing and Compensating Differentials):** Slaughterhouse labor disutility, compensating wage differentials, and hospitality wage competition raising reservation wages, producing a relatively inelastic short-run labor supply curve for local meat processing.

**Microeconomics (Resilience Externality and Public Goods):** Disaster processing readiness functions as an unpriced positive externality. Private processors carry the full cost of facility upkeep and payroll overhead during normal weeks, while the social resilience benefit accrues to the island during port disruptions. Because private firms cannot capture this resilience value in normal retail beef sales, private markets systematically under-provide standby processing capacity.

## 6. Policy Recommendation and Counterarguments

**Proposed Policy:** A County or State Emergency Standby Processing Retainer tied to a funded Butcher Apprenticeship and Retention Compact. The standby retainer directly funds the compensating wage differential needed to retain trained cutters against resort wage competition, tied to a multi-year service commitment to prevent trained labor from exiting to hospitality. During a declared emergency, the retainer funds a surge second shift to expand throughput toward facility nameplate limits.

**Objection 1 (Retainer triggers wage bidding war or loses apprentices to tourism):** Addressed by pairing training subsidies with a contractual service commitment and wage-match incentives tied directly to the readiness retainer.

**Objection 2 (Physical canned or frozen stockpiles are cheaper disaster insurance):** Physical stockpiles suffer from shelf-life expiration, high warehouse footprint costs, and vulnerability to electrical grid collapse for cold storage. In contrast, local herds sustain themselves on pasture as an active biological reserve, and funding processing capacity maintains an active production system that continuously generates edible calories throughout a protracted shipping cutoff.

## 2. Hypothesis Inversion & Structural Reframing
* **Prior Working Hypothesis:** Downstream labor shortages (cutter deficit, wage suppression relative to resort hospitality) and chiller constraints act as the primary bottleneck capping Kauaʻi beef supply.
* **Revised Empirical Hypothesis:** The binding constraint is upstream at the cow-calf junction, driven by the microeconomic opportunity cost and comparative advantage of exporting weaned calves (3,707 head under 500 lbs vs. 1,911 cattle 500+ lbs, USDA 2022 Table 11). Downstream processing currently operates with unutilized capacity (slack).
* **Sequential Dynamic:** Downstream labor and cooler capacity become binding sequentially only after policy successfully alters retention economics and doubles weekly processing demand.
