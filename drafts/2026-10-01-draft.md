KAUA'I BEEF SUPPLY AND CONVERSION CAPACITY: TESTING MIDSTREAM CONSTRAINTS AGAINST UPSTREAM EXPORT REALITIES

## 1. INTRODUCTION AND PROBLEM FRAMING

Kaua'i maintains significant agricultural land resources—encompassing 42,345 acres of pasture land and an aggregate inventory of approximately 13,893 cattle and calves (USDA Census of Agriculture, 2022). Despite this domestic land and livestock base, the county remains integrated into Hawai'i's broader food import paradigm, where an estimated 85% to 90% of total food consumption is supplied via ocean freight (Leung & Loke, 2008).

In public policy discourse, this disparity is frequently attributed to downstream "conversion bottlenecks"—specifically, presumed deficits in slaughterhouse infrastructure, acute shortages of certified meat cutters, inadequate cold-storage/dry-aging space, and scheduling frictions with federal meat inspectors. Under this framing, maritime shipping disruptions are treated as exogenous shocks that cause shortages, rather than stress events that expose existing domestic conversion failures.

To evaluate whether downstream processing capacity acts as the binding constraint on local beef supply, this study benchmarks inspected processing throughput against upstream herd allocations, comparative advantage in finishing, and published agricultural baselines.

## 2. METHODOLOGY & FIELD EVIDENCE

Empirical herd distributions and market flows were established using the USDA 2022 Census of Agriculture (Table 11: Cattle and Calves Inventory and Sales). In Census Table 11, cattle sales are categorized into calves weighing less than 500 pounds and "cattle including calves 500 pounds or more." Regional labor rates were evaluated using Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics (BLS OEWS, May 2025) for the Hawai'i / Kaua'i nonmetropolitan area.

To evaluate physical operational constraints against macroeconomic aggregates, primary operational data were collected via a structured operational audit of an inspected commercial processing facility on Kaua'i (Operations manager, inspected processing facility, personal communication, October 1, 2026), evaluating producer lead times, weekly operating days, cooler utilization, cutting labor structures, and USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) inspection availability.

## 3. EMPIRICAL FINDINGS: TESTING THE CAPACITY HYPOTHESES

### 3.1 Midstream Processing: Current Slack and Sequential Bottlenecks

Field evidence from an inspected facility on Kaua'i indicates that midstream processing capacity is not currently the binding constraint suppressing local beef availability:

- Booking Lead Times and Queue Friction: Independent livestock producers face average scheduling lead times of approximately one week, accommodating commercial operations alongside smallholders harvesting 1 to 2 head annually for home consumption without operational backlog.

- Facility Utilization and Labor Economics: Slaughter and butchering operations currently run two days per week. The facility manager reports that current operating frequency is determined entirely by inbound animal volume rather than physical plant or labor ceilings ("with what we currently have coming in, twice a week is all we need"). While skilled cutters maintain secondary employment, this dual employment reflects labor market adaptation to low slaughter volume, rather than an exogenous labor shortage restricting plant operation. This aligns with BLS OEWS May 2025 wage data: slaughterers and meat packers in the Kaua'i nonmetropolitan area earn a mean hourly wage of $18.90 (mean annual $39,310), lagging competitive entry-level positions in resort hospitality and food service ($20.65 to $26.38). Compensation rates reflect low processing volume demand rather than competitive bidding for scarce labor.

- Cold Storage and Dry-Aging Allocation: Cooler capacity does not restrict weekly slaughter headcount. Extended 30-day dry aging is restricted to a specialized proprietary brand standard; commercial and custom clients specify holding periods between 14 and 28 days based on individual commercial preference without creating cooler displacement.

- Regulatory FSIS Friction: USDA FSIS inspection availability does not constrain operating days. Approximately 99% of total carcasses processed at the facility enter distribution under full USDA inspection, demonstrating that regulatory access does not restrict commercial entry.

### 3.2 The Upstream Structural Divergence: Comparative Advantage and Opportunity Cost

Because the processing facility operates with substantial unutilized slack capacity under current arrival rates, the primary supply constraint occurs upstream at the cow-calf marketing junction.

According to USDA 2022 Census Table 11 data, Kaua'i operations sold 3,707 calves weighing under 500 pounds, alongside 1,911 cattle (including calves 500 pounds or more), representing a combined annual commercial distribution of 5,618 head. Assuming the vast majority of weanlings exit via maritime transport to continental feedlots, approximately 66% of marketed animals leave the island food system prior to the finishing stage. The driver of this divergence is explained by microeconomic opportunity cost and classical comparative advantage:

- Opportunity Cost and Net Realized Returns: A cow-calf operator faces a discrete marginal decision at weaning: liquidate a 450-pound calf for immediate cash settlement via the mainland feeder export market, or retain the animal for an additional 18 to 24 months of pasture finishing on Kaua'i.
  - Exporting Route: Selling an exported 450 lb feeder calf generates immediate revenue based on continental auction spot rates (roughly $2.80/lb to $3.00/lb gross, or $1,260 to $1,350 per head), netting approximately $1,050 to $1,150 after cow-box maritime container freight, insurance, and handling.
  - Local Pasture-Finishing Route: Finishing the steer to a harvest weight of 1,000–1,050 lbs yields roughly 350 lbs of retail cuts (or 600 lbs hanging weight). At a wholesale carcass price of $4.75/lb ($2,850 gross), the rancher must absorb an estimated $1,200 to $1,500 in cumulative pasture carrying costs, drought-reserve forage, mineral supplements, and land-lease overhead over 18 to 24 months, in addition to $350 in harvest/slaughter fees.
  - The net present value of immediate liquidation is competitive with, or superior to, local finishing when adjusted for mortality risk, pasture carrying risk, and working capital interest.

- Comparative Advantage and Factor Endowments: Mainland commercial feedlots possess a comparative advantage in energy-dense, low-cost finishing rations (concentrated corn and grain) and scale efficiencies. In contrast, Kaua'i's agricultural endowment favors extensive cow-calf grazing on sub-tropical perennial pastures. Exporting weaned calves represents an economically rational trade alignment based on factor endowments, leaving the domestic processing sector starved of inbound live animal units.

## 4. POLICY IMPLICATIONS AND THE RESILIENCE EXTERNALITY

### 4.1 The Economic Objection to Production Subsidies

From a pure free-market efficiency perspective, if mainland feedlots finish cattle at a lower marginal cost, subsidizing local pasture finishing or intermediate conversion appears allocatively inefficient. Forcing domestic retention under standard trade theory misallocates land and labor resources away from areas of natural comparative advantage.

### 4.2 The Resilience Externality Defense

The justification for policy intervention rests entirely on a positive externality: island food system resilience. Because 85% to 90% of food enters via ocean freight (Leung & Loke, 2008), the social value of maintaining active local finishing herds and viable slaughter infrastructure exceeds private market prices during shipping interruptions or geopolitical logistics failures. When supply chains sever, market-dependent populations face severe supply inelasticity.

### 4.3 Sequential Policy Architecture

Because private ranching operations cannot capture this resilience value in spot auction prices, targeted intervention is required to bridge the margin gap between export liquidation and local finishing:

- Institutional Forward-Contracting: Long-term procurement agreements from public institutions (e.g., public school food programs, regional hospitals) that establish floor prices pegged above mainland net-back feeder prices, compensating ranchers for local finishing carrying risk.
- Risk-Mitigation Working Capital: Establishing low-interest operating credit lines that finance the 18-to-24-month working capital cycle required to hold weaned calves through grass-finishing stages.
- Sequential Capacity Management: State policy must recognize the sequential nature of supply chain bottlenecks. Capital grants directed toward expanding physical slaughterhouse footprint or unneeded cold storage are premature while the facility operates with slack capacity at two days per week. However, if retention policies succeed in redirecting even half of Kaua'i's 3,707 exported calves (~35 additional head per week), plant throughput would roughly double from current slaughter levels (~37 head/week to ~72 head/week). At that threshold, cutting labor shifts and chiller rotation schedules would rapidly transition from slack to the binding constraint. Public capital investment must therefore be structured as a phased secondary trigger contingent upon verified increases in upstream cattle retention.

## 5. CONCLUSION

Primary operational evidence combined with agricultural census data indicates that Kaua'i's beef supply deficit is not currently caused by midstream slaughterhouse bottlenecks, regulatory inspection shortages, or cold-storage ceilings. The island's processing infrastructure operates below capacity due to insufficient animal deliveries. Interventions aimed strictly at subsidizing slaughter facilities fail to address the core economic mechanism: the superior risk-adjusted opportunity cost of exporting weanlings to mainland grain finish facilities. Meaningful food security policy must focus first on closing the margin gap between calf exports and domestic cattle retention, while scheduling conversion infrastructure investments as a sequential secondary requirement once upstream retention targets are achieved.
