KAUA'I BEEF SUPPLY AND CONVERSION CAPACITY: TESTING MIDSTREAM CONSTRAINTS AGAINST UPSTREAM EXPORT REALITIES

## 1. INTRODUCTION AND PROBLEM FRAMING

Kaua'i maintains significant agricultural land resources—encompassing 42,345 acres of pasture land (Hawai'i Department of Agriculture [HDOA], 2020) and an aggregate herd inventory of 13,893 cattle and calves (USDA Census of Agriculture, 2022). Despite this domestic land and livestock base, the county remains integrated into Hawai'i's broader food import paradigm, where an estimated 85% to 90% of total food consumption is supplied via ocean freight (Leung & Loke, 2008).

In public policy discourse, this disparity is frequently attributed to downstream "conversion bottlenecks"—specifically, presumed deficits in slaughterhouse infrastructure, acute shortages of certified meat cutters, inadequate cold-storage/dry-aging space, and scheduling frictions with federal meat inspectors. Under this framing, maritime shipping disruptions are treated as exogenous shocks that cause shortages, rather than stress events that expose existing domestic conversion failures.

To evaluate whether downstream processing capacity acts as the primary constraint on local beef supply, this study benchmarks inspected processing throughput against upstream herd allocations, comparative advantage in finishing, and published agricultural baselines.

## 2. METHODOLOGY & FIELD EVIDENCE

Herd distributions and regional labor rates were established using the USDA 2022 Census of Agriculture and BLS OEWS May 2025 nonmetropolitan data. Primary midstream operational constraints were identified via audits of Kaua'i's main multi-user inspected processing facility (Operations manager, personal communications, October 1 and 2, 2026). According to the operations manager, other island facilities serve closed retail channels or remain inactive for regular commercial slaughter, positioning the audited plant as the primary gateway for independent producers.

## 3. EMPIRICAL FINDINGS: TESTING THE CAPACITY HYPOTHESES

### 3.1 Midstream Processing: Shallow Slack and Fixed Infrastructure Ceilings

Field evidence from the inspected facility indicates that processing capacity currently exhibits operational slack, but that this slack is remarkably shallow and constrained by fixed infrastructure rather than regulatory inspection:

- Queue Friction & Utilization: Independent producers face ~1-week lead times. Management reports slaughtering ~8 head on a Wednesday shift and a similarly sized weekend shift, totaling an observed volume of ~16 head per week. Operating frequency is governed strictly by inbound deliveries.

- The Shallow Capacity Ceiling & Fixed Costs: The facility's maximum throughput under existing infrastructure is ~25 head per week, leaving only ~9 head of unutilized slack. Expanding beyond this ceiling is restricted by physical capital and high fixed operating costs rather than simple scheduling. The operational bottleneck is governed by:
  1. Cooler Rail Capacity: Cooler space is limited by physical hanging rail footage, which caps carcass holding during peak weekend slaughter.
  2. Environmental and Utility Overhead: Higher volume exponentially increases wastewater generation, requiring frequent commercial holding tank pumping, waste hauling, and substantial electricity loads for continuous refrigeration.
  3. Remote Maintenance Friction: Critical mechanical assets (saws, hoists, refrigeration compressors) face accelerated wear under higher throughput. Sourcing commercial parts and certified technicians requires off-island maritime or air freight, imposing lumpy maintenance overhead that cannot be absorbed without consistent volume.

- Labor Structure and Wage Disparities: Butchering labor operates on a part-time basis because workers maintain primary employment elsewhere. Expanding slaughter from a part-time morning shift to full-time industrial operations requires offering competitive compensation. According to BLS OEWS May 2025 data, slaughterers and meat packers in the Hawai'i / Kaua'i nonmetropolitan area earn a median hourly wage of $18.90 (median annual $39,310; mean hourly $19.91, mean annual $41,410), trailing entry-level positions in resort hospitality and food service ($20.65 to $26.38 median hourly). At current low volumes, fixed processing revenue cannot support full-time wage premiums.

- Regulatory FSIS Friction: USDA FSIS inspection availability does not constrain operating days. Approximately 99% of total carcasses processed at the facility enter distribution under full USDA inspection, demonstrating that regulatory access does not restrict commercial entry.

### 3.2 The Upstream Structural Divergence: Comparative Advantage and Opportunity Cost

Because the processing facility operates with unutilized capacity under current delivery levels, the initial supply constraint occurs upstream at the cow-calf marketing junction.

According to USDA 2022 Census Table 11 data, Kaua'i operations marketed 3,707 calves weighing under 500 pounds, alongside 1,911 cattle (including calves 500 pounds or more), representing a combined annual commercial distribution of 5,618 head. Calves under 500 pounds represent approximately 66% of all marketed bovine animals, with an annualized outflow of roughly 71.3 calves per week leaving the island. The driver of this divergence is explained by microeconomic opportunity cost, discounted cash flows, and classical comparative advantage:

- Opportunity Cost and Risk-Adjusted Net Present Value: A cow-calf operator faces a discrete marginal decision at weaning: liquidate a 450-pound calf for immediate cash settlement via the continental feeder export market, or retain the animal for an additional 18 to 24 months of pasture finishing on Kaua'i.
  - Route A (Continental Feeder Export): Historically tight national cattle inventories pushed continental feeder auction prices for 400-500 lb Medium & Large 1 steers to between $4.70/lb and $5.47/lb ($470.00 to $547.50/cwt) in October 2026 (USDA Agricultural Marketing Service [AMS], Report SJ_LS850, October 2, 2026). At a conservative benchmark of $5.00/lb, a 450 lb calf generates an immediate gross revenue of $2,250. Deducting maritime freight, insurance, and mainland handling costs (assumed at ~$200 per head), the rancher nets an immediate cash settlement of approximately $2,050 per head at Year 0.
  - Route B (Local Pasture Finishing): Retaining the animal to a live weight of 1,000 lbs yields a 560 lb hanging carcass (56% dressing yield) and approximately 350 lbs of retail cuts (62.5% fabrication yield). At an average wholesale hanging price of $4.75/lb (Operations manager, personal communication, October 1, 2026), gross carcass revenue equals $2,660. Deducting an inspected slaughter and processing fee of $350 (Operations manager, personal communication, October 1, 2026) and estimated pasture carrying costs of $1,200 to $1,400 over 24 months (assuming $50–$58/month in forage management, land leases, mineral supplements, and veterinary care), the nominal undiscounted margin ranges from $910 to $1,110.
  - Time and Risk Discounting: The returns from Route B arrive 24 months after weaning. Discounting the nominal return over two years at an 8% annual cost of capital and factoring in an assumed 3% cumulative herd mortality risk over the pasture cycle ($1 / 1.08^2 * 0.97 = 0.832$), the risk-adjusted Net Present Value (NPV) of local finishing is $757 to $923 per head.

Comparing the immediate export net settlement (~$2,050) against the discounted local finishing NPV ($757 to $923) demonstrates an opportunity-cost deficit of $1,127 to $1,293 in present-value terms. Scaled to nominal terms at the 24-month harvest endpoint, bridging this gap requires an additional $1,354 to $1,554 per head in gross carcass revenue—an incremental price premium of $2.41 to $2.77 per pound on a 560-pound hanging carcass.

- Comparative Advantage and Factor Endowments: Continental commercial feedlots possess a comparative advantage in energy-dense, low-cost finishing rations (concentrated grain) and scale efficiencies. In contrast, Kaua'i's agricultural endowment favors extensive cow-calf grazing on sub-tropical perennial pastures. The export of "wean-offs" represents an economically rational trade alignment based on factor endowments, leaving the domestic processing sector starved of inbound volume.

## 4. POLICY IMPLICATIONS AND THE RESILIENCE EXTERNALITY

### 4.1 The Economic Objection to Production Subsidies

From a pure free-market efficiency perspective, subsidizing local pasture finishing is allocatively inefficient. Bridging an opportunity-cost gap of ~$1,450 per head to retain one-third of Kaua'i's exported calves (~1,236 head annually) requires an annual public subsidy of approximately $1.79 million. Critics rightfully argue that this represents a substantial market distortion to pay for domestic production when continental markets clear at higher margins.

### 4.2 The Resilience Externality Defense

The justification for this intervention rests entirely on a positive externality: island food system resilience. Because 85% to 90% of food enters via ocean freight (Leung & Loke, 2008), the social value of maintaining active local finishing herds exceeds private market prices. While a $1.79 million annual subsidy is undeniably a market distortion, it is a distortion justified by the risk diversification it buys.

Beyond immediate logistical disruptions, domestic agricultural processing mitigates the severe macroeconomic vulnerability of single-sector concentration. Kaua'i's economy is heavily leveraged on hospitality and tourism. When this sector halts—as evidenced by Kaua'i County's unemployment rate surging to approximately 34.4% in April 2020 during pandemic lockdowns (DBEDT, 2020)—the localized economic contraction is immediate. Subsidizing a "walking reserve" of live cattle provides a small but vital non-tourism source of local ranching revenue and processing employment that remains viable during hospitality collapses.

This macroeconomic reality also dictates the policy choice of institutional buyers for Phase 1 forward contracts: unlike resort demand for premium local beef, public school and hospital food budgets remain stable during a tourism crash, providing a truly resilient anchor for the supply chain.

### 4.3 The Sequential Policy Architecture and Coordination Problem

Because private ranching operations cannot capture this resilience value in spot auction prices, policy must address a classic economic coordination problem: ranchers will not retain calves without guaranteed slaughter access, while processors cannot justify high fixed-capital investments in wastewater, rail expansion, and full-time labor without guaranteed animal volume.

Interventions must be deployed sequentially across the supply chain:

- Phase 1: Institutional Forward-Contracting and Working Capital: Long-term procurement agreements from public institutions (e.g., public school food programs, regional hospitals) that establish floor prices pegged between $7.16 and $7.52/lb carcass weight (up from the baseline $4.75/lb). This structure offsets the $2.41 to $2.77/lb opportunity-cost deficit, absorbing carrying risk for the rancher. In parallel, low-interest revolving credit lines must finance the 24-month working capital cycle required to hold weaned calves through grass-finishing stages.
- Phase 2: Immediate Sequential Infrastructure Grants: The facility's 25-head weekly ceiling means operational slack is extremely shallow (~9 head/week). The plant reaches its absolute ceiling if ranchers retain just 13% of exported calves (~9 additional head per week). Any policy aiming for higher retention (e.g., one-fourth of exports, pushing total volume to ~34 head/week) will immediately exhaust midstream capacity. Capital grants targeted at wastewater infrastructure and expanded cooler rail capacity must therefore be funded simultaneously with Phase 1 retention incentives, ensuring midstream limits do not choke off upstream retention.

## 5. CONCLUSION

Primary operational evidence combined with agricultural census data indicates that Kaua'i's beef supply deficit is initiated upstream by the microeconomic opportunity cost of exporting weaned calves to continental markets. However, the midstream processing sector does not possess indefinite slack. While currently operating below its physical ceiling due to a lack of animal deliveries, the island's inspected processing capacity is bounded by a shallow ceiling of approximately 25 head per week, constrained by fixed wastewater, chiller rail, and maintenance overhead. Addressing island beef food security requires a coordinated sequential strategy: funding forward contracts to bridge the upstream opportunity-cost gap, while simultaneously deploying targeted capital grants to expand midstream physical infrastructure before retention volume collides with the facility's capacity ceiling.
